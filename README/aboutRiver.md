The biggest improvement I would make is this:

Don't make “Why River” and “Our Mission” feel like corporate-information sections. They should answer the visitor's questions progressively:

What is River? → Why does River exist? → Who is behind it? → Where did it come from? → What does River believe?

Your proposed sections can absolutely do that.

The About River story
01 — Why River

This should be the opening story, and I think the name itself is worth explaining.

River is a powerful metaphor for the company because it naturally carries several ideas:

Water — essential, adaptable, and fundamental to life.
Life — rivers sustain communities, ecosystems and growth.
Movement — a river doesn't remain still; it continuously moves forward.
Adaptability — it changes course around obstacles without losing its destination.
Persistence — it keeps moving, even when the path becomes difficult.
Refreshing — it brings renewal and creates new possibilities.
Relentlessness — it continues toward its destination.

But I wouldn't make this sound poetic for the sake of being poetic.

The metaphor should connect directly to how River operates.

Something like the underlying idea:

We chose the name River because a river is always moving. It adapts to its environment, finds a way around obstacles and continues toward its destination. It sustains life, creates possibilities and transforms the landscape around it.

That is the kind of company we want River to be.

That gives you an emotional beginning before you move into the more rational parts of the company.

02 — Our Mission

Then answer:

Why does River exist?

This is where I would strongly connect River's mission to problems rather than technology.

River isn't here simply to:

build websites, apps, software or digital products.

Those are outputs.

The deeper purpose is:

to help organizations solve problems, improve how they operate and create better outcomes through technology.

And this is where you can introduce the different types of problems River addresses:

We build when something doesn't exist.

We improve when something exists but isn't performing well.

We move when technology or data needs to change environments.

We connect when systems need to work together.

We protect when technology needs greater security, resilience or continuity.

We assess when the right answer isn't yet clear.

Then bring it to the fruits/results you mentioned.

Not:

“We deliver software.”

But:

Better systems. Better operations. Better experiences. Greater resilience. New capabilities. Measurable progress.

That makes the mission much more meaningful.

03 — Our Team

This section should answer:

What kind of people are River?

This is an opportunity to define your culture without falling into generic corporate language.

I would avoid things like:

“We are passionate, innovative, dedicated professionals.”

Almost every technology company says that.

Instead, define the character of the people you want at River.

For example:

Curious

We want to understand how things actually work.

Analytical

We don't jump to solutions before understanding the problem.

Practical

Good engineering has to work in the real world.

Responsible

We take ownership of what we build and the consequences of our decisions.

Persistent

Complex problems rarely yield immediately.

Collaborative

Great systems are rarely created by one discipline or one person.

Always learning

Technology changes. Our thinking has to change with it.

This becomes your people philosophy, rather than merely an employee description.

04 — Meet the Team

Then let the visitor meet the actual people.

Your idea of JS-rendered team cards is good.

I would make this feel less like a traditional:

“Our Leadership Team”

and more like:

The people behind River.

Each card could contain:

Photo
Name
Role
Discipline / speciality
Short personal statement
LinkedIn or other relevant link
Optional “Ask me about...” line

And because you're already thinking about central registries for Projects and Media, I'd recommend treating the team in a similar way:

one team registry → multiple components can consume it.

That means the About page isn't hardcoded every time someone joins or changes roles.

05 — Our Origin

This is where your founder story belongs.

And yes, I strongly recommend including the electrical & electronics engineering background.

But this should come after visitors understand what River stands for.

Then the story becomes much more powerful.

The structure should be:

Engineering foundation

Our founder's background in electrical and electronics engineering established an engineering mindset rooted in systems thinking, understanding how things work, solving complex problems and working within constraints.

Experience

Working across different organizations exposed our founder to different technologies, operations, business environments and real-world challenges.

And this is where the story gets interesting.

The experience revealed something:

Organizations rarely need technology for technology's sake.

They need technology to solve something.

A process is too slow.

A system doesn't communicate with another system.

Data is trapped somewhere.

Infrastructure isn't reliable.

An existing product isn't performing.

A business has an idea but doesn't know how to turn it into a working system.

Technology needs to respond to those problems.

The realization

Then comes your turning point:

Technology is most valuable when it is deliberately engineered around the problem it is meant to solve.

That's the philosophy that eventually became River.

The founding

Then:

River was established to bring an engineering-led approach to technology — beginning with the problem, understanding the environment, determining what is actually needed, and engineering a solution that works in the real world.

And then you connect it directly to what River is today.

I would actually add one more section

There's one thing missing from your structure.

After Our Origin, I would add:

06 — What We Believe

Because the origin explains where River came from.

The mission explains why River exists.

The team explains who River is made of.

But you still need to explain:

What principles guide the decisions River makes?

This is where you can establish your engineering philosophy.

For example:

Problems before technology.
We start by understanding what needs to change.

Purpose before complexity.
We don't add technology simply because we can.

Engineering over decoration.
A system should work well, not merely look impressive.

Evidence over assumptions.
We test, measure and learn.

Build for reality.
Solutions must work within actual budgets, people, infrastructure and constraints.

Think beyond launch.
A system isn't finished simply because it has gone live.

This would make the About page significantly stronger.

The most important thing

I would not make the About page say:

“Here is our company. Here are our values. Here is our founder. Here is our team.”

Instead, make it read like a journey:

There is a reason we are called River.
↓
There is a problem we exist to solve.
↓
There is a particular kind of person we believe should solve it.
↓
There is a story that led us here.
↓
There are principles that guide how we work.
↓
And this is what River is building today.

That would make the About page feel much more like the story and philosophy of an engineering company, rather than a conventional “About Us” page.

And it fits the River website strategy you've been developing extremely well: Products show what River can repeatedly package and build; Services show how River helps organizations; Projects prove what River has actually solved; About explains why River thinks and works the way it does.
