Yes. For River’s Products page, I would make it significantly more strategic than a normal “here are our products” page.

Your Projects & Case Studies page already proves what River has built.
The Products page should prove what River can repeatedly package, sell, deploy, and scale.

My recommended structure
1. Hero — “Products, Not Just Projects”

Don't lead with a generic:

Our Products

Instead, position River as a company that creates usable software products and systems.

Example positioning:

Software Products Built to Solve Real Problems
From business automation and customer platforms to internal tools and industry-specific systems, River builds products designed around how organizations actually operate.

Then have two clear actions:

Explore Products
Build a Product With Us
2. Product Categories

This should be the first major section.

I'd recommend categories such as:

🟢 Business & Enterprise

🟢 Customer & Commerce

🟢 Operations & Productivity

Don't create dozens of empty products just to fill the page. Start with products you can actually demonstrate.

3. Product Cards — Make Them Feel Like Real Software

This is probably the most important upgrade.

Each product shouldn't just be:

Inventory System
A system for managing inventory.

Instead, make every card feel like a miniature SaaS/product landing page.

For example:

InventoryOS

Inventory Management Platform

Control stock, suppliers, purchasing, movements and reporting from one centralized platform.

Core capabilities

Inventory Suppliers Purchasing Reports Users

Then:

Status: 🟢 Available
Deployment: Cloud / Private Server
Model: Subscription / Custom Deployment

Buttons:

View Product
Request Demo

Give major products their own route:

5. “Problem → Product → Outcome”

This is where your Products page can become much stronger than competitors' pages.

Instead of simply saying:

We built an inventory system.

Show:

THE PROBLEM

Businesses lose visibility when stock, purchasing and sales information are scattered across spreadsheets and disconnected systems.

↓

THE PRODUCT

InventoryOS centralizes stock, purchasing, suppliers and reporting.

↓

THE OUTCOME

One operational view of inventory, with automated workflows and real-time reporting.

That connects directly with the positioning we discussed for your Projects & Case Studies page.

8. SaaS vs Custom Deployment

This is especially important for a software company.

Don't make every product look like something the client must purchase off-the-shelf.

Give visitors three paths:

🟢 Ready Product

Deploy an existing River product.

🟢 Customize

Adapt an existing product to your organization's workflow.

🟢 Build From Scratch

Develop a completely custom platform around your requirements.

This creates a natural bridge between your Products and Services pages.

9. Product Comparison / Selector

I'd also add:

“What are you trying to solve?”

The visitor selects one → River recommends the appropriate product/service.

That's much better UX than forcing visitors to understand your internal product categories.

Optimized frontend, backend and data operations.

Deployment

Cloud, private infrastructure or hybrid environments.

This is especially valuable for potential corporate clients.

14. “Built by River” Section

I would also make a distinction between:

River Products

Products that River owns/develops as reusable solutions.

and

Custom systems River has built for individual clients.

This is important because you don't want visitors wondering:

“Are these products actually yours, or are these just client projects?”

Your Projects page answers “What have you built?”

Your Products page answers:

“What software solutions does River offer?”

🟢 2. Product categories

🟢 4. High-quality product cards
🟢 5. Product detail pages
🟢 6. Custom media-player integration
🟢 7. Problem → Product → Outcome
🟢 8. Product selector
🟢 9. Technical architecture/trust section
🟢 10. Product → Case Study linking
🟢 11. Deployment/pricing model
🟢 12. Strong product-development CTA

The key principle: your Projects page should prove “we have built it,” while your Products page should make the visitor think “River has something that can solve my problem.”

That distinction will make the Products page much more commercially powerful.
